# Content, intent and AI search

## Page usefulness and opportunities

Start from the intended visitor's task and page type. Inspect whether the page
answers that task accurately, supplies appropriate evidence/examples, explains
the offer and provides a working next step. Check title, headings, snippet
description, internal links, accessible media and the actual destination.
Record misleading, duplicated or missing content with page-specific examples.

Use the helpful-content questions: who produced it, how the knowledge/result was
obtained and why the page helps its audience. Verify claimed experience,
credentials, sources and dates where readers rely on them. E-E-A-T is a quality
assessment lens, not an observable Google score. Do not turn word count,
keyword density, a single-H1 rule or fixed internal-link quotas into ranking
requirements. A short product page can satisfy its task; longer text alone does
not repair weak evidence. Use
[Google's people-first guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content).

For a content/competitor gap request:

1. State audience, task, market/language, query set and observation date. Separate
   proposed queries from observed searches and measured demand. If search access
   is missing, keep the comparison provisional rather than inventing rankings.
2. Sample relevant results/pages with their actual format, answer, evidence,
   product fit and observed gaps. Do not infer competitor traffic, weakness or
   conversion from a title alone. Retain result context and source URLs.
3. Map related queries to existing pages by user task; check whether revision or
   consolidation is more useful than another near-duplicate page. Similar
   keywords do not prove cannibalization without page/query evidence.
4. Return a bounded opportunity set and, when requested, briefs with audience,
   intent, target page, source-backed claims, original contribution, outline,
   internal links, honest CTA and validation. Refresh actual product/pricing
   facts before a comparison; distinguish observed facts from positioning.

Hand production/acquisition experiments to `product-growth` when available. Use
the existing writing/media owners for the chosen format. Preserve source and
claim evidence through revisions; a brief is not a finished asset or publication.

## AI search visibility

Treat GEO/AEO as a question about a named search/answer surface and observed
evidence. Google's
[AI feature guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features)
requires no special AI text file or schema. Other vendors have their own access
and presentation behavior; verify the relevant vendor's current documentation
before attributing a result to a crawler rule.

Keep these checks separate:

- **Access/eligibility:** actual response, crawler-specific rules, index/snippet
  controls and rendering evidence. Training crawlers, search crawlers and
  user-triggered fetchers can have different policies. Allowing one does not
  guarantee a citation; changing that policy needs target-specific authority.
- **Content quality:** clear entities, supported answers, current original
  evidence and useful context. Specific formatting may help readers; it is not
  proof of an AI ranking mechanism.
- **Observed appearance:** engine/surface, query or prompt, date, market,
  account/personalization context, sampled answer and cited URL. A mention
  without a source link is different from a citation. A bounded spot check is
  not stable share of voice, and non-observation is not proof of exclusion.
- **Referrals/outcomes:** actual attributable visits/actions from permitted data.
  An answer mentioning a brand does not prove a visit, lead or sale.

Treat `llms.txt`, agent-readiness scores and emerging agent protocols as optional
experiments with named consumers and measurable hypotheses, never established
Google inclusion requirements. Preserve training/access preferences; do not
auto-allow bots, generate mass mentions or create fabricated independent reviews.
